Hypophosphatemia (<0.81 mmol/L)

Key Causes:

  • Cellular Redistribution: Refeeding syndrome, insulin therapy, respiratory alkalosis, catecholamines.
  • Increased Renal Loss: Diuretics, Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT), osmotic diuresis (e.g., in DKA).
  • Decreased Absorption: Malnutrition, vomiting, diarrhea, use of phosphate binders.

Primary Mechanism of Injury:

Depletion of intracellular ATP (cellular energy failure) and 2,3-DPG (impaired oxygen delivery), leading to multi-organ dysfunction.

Management Pearls:

  • Administer IV phosphate slowly (over 4-8 hours).
  • Check and correct concurrent hypomagnesemia.
  • Anticipate and prevent in high-risk patients (e.g., refeeding).

Hyperphosphatemia (>1.45 mmol/L)

Key Causes:

  • Decreased Renal Excretion: Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) is the number one cause.
  • Massive Phosphate Load: Rhabdomyolysis, tumor lysis syndrome, massive hemolysis.
  • Exogenous Sources: Massive blood transfusions, phosphate-containing laxatives, over-repletion.

Primary Mechanism of Injury:

Complexing with serum calcium, leading to acute hypocalcemia and deposition of calcium-phosphate crystals in soft tissues, causing vascular calcification and organ damage.

Management Pearls:

  • Treat the underlying cause (e.g., manage AKI).
  • Renal Replacement Therapy (RRT) is the definitive treatment.
  • Avoid IV calcium unless patient is symptomatic from hypocalcemia.

Visual Summary: The Infographic

The U-Shaped Risk Curve

Large observational studies reveal a critical insight: both low and high serum phosphate levels are independently associated with an increased risk of death in the ICU. This highlights the importance of maintaining phosphate within the normal physiological range.

Prevalence in High-Risk Populations

While common in the general ICU population, the incidence of hypophosphatemia is significantly higher in specific subgroups. Patients with sepsis, trauma, or those undergoing CRRT are particularly vulnerable and require vigilant monitoring.
